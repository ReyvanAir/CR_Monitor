Pins & wiring

Every connection, and why it lands where it does

The ESP32-S3 DevKitC-1 N16R8 advertises 45 usable GPIOs. By the time the flash, the PSRAM, the USB pair, the serial console and the ADC2 restriction have taken their share, the display's thirteen signals fit with almost nothing to spare. Nearly every pin choice on this board is forced. This page records what forced it.

Before you wire anything

Both boards present male pins, so every jumper here is female to female. Shield labels are read from its own silkscreen (headers J1 to J4); ESP32 numbers are the GPIO numbers printed on the DevKitC-1.

The pin budget

Four constraints eliminate most of the map before any design choice is made:

  • GPIO 26 to 32 are the SPI flash and 33 to 37 are the OPI PSRAM, which is what the "R8" in N16R8 means. Twelve pins gone, permanently.
  • GPIO 22 to 25 do not exist on the S3 package. They are absent, not reserved.
  • GPIO 43 and 44 carry UART0, which is the serial CSV output; 19 and 20 are USB; 0, 45 and 46 are strapping pins sampled at reset.
  • ADC2 stops converting whenever Wi-Fi is on. Anything analogue has to sit on ADC1, meaning GPIO 1 to 10.

Do not run the MAX4466 from 5 V

Its output centres at half its supply rail. On 5 V that puts the quiescent point at 2.5 V and swings peaks past the 3.3 V the ESP32 ADC can accept, which can damage the pin. On 3.3 V the centre is 1.65 V, roughly 2048 counts, which is exactly what the boot self-test checks for.

Why LCD_RS and LCD_CS are pinned to GPIO 2 and 1

On a resistive touch panel these two lines are also the touch layer's XM and YP electrodes, and reading a touch means calling analogRead() on them. That makes them analogue inputs, which puts them under the same ADC1 requirement as the microphone: ADC2 is unavailable while Wi-Fi is running. GPIO 1 and 2 are the only low-numbered pins still free once the mic, buzzer and I²C bus have taken 5, 7, 8 and 9.

The regulator problem

Investigation closed 2026-09-13

For a while this display did not work, and the symptom was misleading: the backlight lit normally, the screen stayed permanently white, and reading the controller ID back over the bus returned 0x00 or 0xFF. A lit backlight makes the board look alive, which sent the search toward the driver and the pin map before the power rail.

The backlight runs directly from 5 V. The controller and its bus buffers do not. On a shield with an onboard 3.3 V regulator the J4 3.3V pin is an output and you leave it alone; on a shield without one it is an input, and if you leave it alone the entire logic domain has no supply at all. That is the state this board was in.

The test

  1. Wire 5V and GND only. Nothing else.
  2. Power the ESP32 over USB.
  3. Measure the shield's 3.3V pin against GND.
ReadingMeaningAction
~3.3 VOnboard regulator presentLeave 3.3V unconnected.
0 VNo regulatorWire shield 3.3V to ESP32 3V3.

Result on this board: no regulator

A visual inspection found no AMS1117 or any other 3.3 V part. The only two ICs are U1 and U2, both marked SM245TC, which are octal bus transceivers. So J4 3.3V is an input and must be wired, and the shield's whole logic domain including those buffers sits at 3.3 V.

That last detail also settles a question worth raising on any parallel shield. A plain HC245 running from 5 V has an input threshold near 3.5 V, which the S3's 3.3 V drive would meet only marginally. Here the buffers are powered from the same 3.3 V rail the ESP32 drives, so the levels match by construction and the concern does not apply. If you are wiring a different board, read the markings on its buffers first: LVC245 or ALC245 is fine on 3.3 V logic, and a 5 V-powered HC245 is a reason to stop and reconsider.

Wiring practice

This is a parallel bus with no error checking. Signal integrity problems do not announce themselves; they show up as occasional wrong pixels, which is easy to blame on software.

  • Keep the eight data wires, and LCD_WR above all, short and roughly equal in length. Long floppy jumpers on the write strobe produce glitchy pixels.
  • Connect both grounds. Thirteen fast-switching lines need a return path, and a single thin ground wire is not one.
  • Budget for the backlight. 120 to 150 mA is comfortable over USB, but leave headroom when moving to a wall adapter.
  • Do not disturb GPIO 5, 7, 8, 9, 43 or 44 while rearranging the display wiring. Those are the mic, the buzzer, the I²C bus and the serial console.

Confirm the bus before flashing the full sketch

Test_TFT/Test_TFT.ino exists for this. A red screen means the pin map and the bus are correct. White or blank means checking LCD_WR, LCD_CS, LCD_RST and the 3.3 V question, in that order, before suspecting anything in the firmware.
